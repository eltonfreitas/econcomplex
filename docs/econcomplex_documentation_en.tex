\section{Overview}

\pkg{econcomplex} is a Python library that consolidates the best
implementations of Economic Complexity and Regional Science indicators,
combining functionalities from four reference packages in the literature:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{EconGeo} (Balland, 2017) --- R package focused on geographic
        and regional specialization indicators
  \item \textbf{economiccomplexity} (Vargas Sep\'{u}lveda, 2020) --- R package
        with C++ backend (Armadillo) for complexity indicators
  \item \textbf{py-ecomplexity} (The Growth Lab at Harvard, 2020) --- complete
        Python pipeline with time series support
  \item \textbf{py-economic-complexity} --- modular Python implementation
        with Polars support and cross-space indicators
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Installation}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=bashstyle,
  caption={Installation via pip}]
# From PyPI (recommended)
pip install econcomplex

# Optional: prediction models (adds scikit-learn)
pip install "econcomplex[ml]"

# Latest development version, directly from GitHub
pip install git+https://github.com/eltonfreitas/econcomplex.git

# Or, for local development, from the library root directory
pip install -e .

# Verify the installation
python3 -c "import econcomplex as ec; print(ec.__version__)"
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{API Map: Entry Points, Advanced Implementations, and Aliases}

The public API has three layers. \textbf{Entry points} are what most
users need; \textbf{advanced implementations} are the method-specific
functions the entry points delegate to (exposed for research use);
\textbf{aliases} are alternative short names bound to the very same
function (no duplicated code or computation).

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\small
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.2cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{10.4cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Entry point} & \textbf{Delegates to (advanced)} \\
\midrule
\texttt{eci\_pci(method=...)} & \texttt{eci\_pci\_eigenvector},
  \texttt{method\_of\_reflections}, \texttt{fitness\_complexity}
  (variants: \texttt{mor\_regions}, \texttt{mor\_activities}) \\

  \texttt{correlation\_proximity} \\

  \texttt{eci\_pci}, \texttt{proximity}, \texttt{density}, COI/COG \\

\end{center}

\section{Data Organisation}

This section describes the input format expected by the library,
ensuring that data is correctly structured before running any indicator.

% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- --
\subsection{Standard Format: Long Table (\textit{long format})}

The \pkg{econcomplex} library works exclusively with
\textbf{tables in long format} (\textit{tidy data}),
where each row represents \textbf{a unique combination} of
region $\times$ activity (and period, when applicable):

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{region} & \textbf{activity} & \textbf{value} & \textbf{year (optional)} \\
\midrule
\texttt{SP}  & \texttt{cnae\_10} & 12\,345 & 2022 \\
\texttt{SP}  & \texttt{cnae\_25} &  6\,789 & 2022 \\
\texttt{RJ}  & \texttt{cnae\_10} &  9\,012 & 2022 \\
\texttt{RJ}  & \texttt{cnae\_25} &  3\,456 & 2022 \\
\texttt{SP}  & \texttt{cnae\_10} & 13\,200 & 2023 \\
$\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{warnbox}
  The library does \textbf{not} accept pivoted matrices (regions in rows,
  activities in columns). Use \fn{pivot\_to\_matrix()} only for
  low-level functions that explicitly require the matrix.
\end{warnbox}

% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- --
\subsection{Data Quality Rules}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{No duplicate rows:} each (\textit{region},
        \textit{activity}) pair must appear exactly once per period.
  \item \textbf{Non-negative values:} the value column must contain
        only numbers $\geq 0$.
  \item \textbf{No \texttt{NaN}:} remove or impute missing values before
        passing the DataFrame.
  \item \textbf{Consistent granularity:} all regions must use
        the same geographic level (e.g., all municipalities
        \textit{or} all states).
  \item \textbf{Homogeneous activities:} use a single classification
        per analysis (e.g., CNAE 2-digit \textit{or} CNAE 4-digit,
        not mixed).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{notebox}
  The library enforces rules 2 and 3: a matrix with negative, \texttt{NaN}
  or infinite cells raises a \texttt{ValueError} that says how many cells
  are affected. Auxiliary inputs given as labelled pandas objects (a GDP or
  population \texttt{Series}, a PCI vector, a proximity matrix) are matched
  to the matrix \textbf{by label}, so their order does not matter and extra
  labels are ignored; a label of the matrix that is absent raises an error
  naming it. Lists, arrays and \texttt{Series} with a default integer index
  are taken by position.
\end{notebox}

% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- --
\subsection{Column Names --- \texttt{cols} Dictionary}

The pipeline uses a \argm{cols} dictionary to map the actual column
names of your DataFrame:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Key} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
\texttt{"loc"}  & required & Region / location column \\
\texttt{"act"}  & required & Activity / product / technology column \\
\texttt{"val"}  & required & Numeric value column (jobs, exports \ldots) \\
\texttt{"time"} & optional & Period column (year, quarter \ldots) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Verifying and preparing the DataFrame before the pipeline}]
import pandas as pd
import econcomplex as ec

df = pd.read_csv("my_data.csv")

# Check structure
print(df.dtypes)
print(df.shape)
print(df.isnull().sum())        # check for NaN
print(df.duplicated().sum())    # check for duplicates

# Basic cleaning
df = df.dropna(subset=["region", "cnae", "jobs"])
df = df[df["jobs"] >= 0]        # remove negative values
df = df.drop_duplicates(subset=["region", "cnae", "year"])

# Run the pipeline
result = ec.compute_complexity(
    df,
    cols={"loc": "region", "act": "cnae",
          "val": "jobs", "time": "year"},
)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Supported File Formats}

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{lll}

                   usecols=["state", "sector", "year", "jobs"])
\end{lstlisting}

% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- --
\subsection{Examples of Compatible Datasets}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em, style=nextline]
  \item[\textbf{RAIS/MTE}]
    Formal employment by municipality $\times$ CNAE (2 or 4 digits)
    $\times$ year. Value column: active employment bonds on 31/12.
  \item[\textbf{COMEX/MDIC}]
    Exports by municipality $\times$ NCM $\times$ year.
    Value column: \texttt{VL\_FOB} (value in USD).
  \item[\textbf{INPI --- Patents}]
    Patent applications by State of the Federation $\times$
    IPC classification $\times$ year.
  \item[\textbf{Atlas of Economic Complexity (Harvard)}]
    Exports by country $\times$ product (HS) $\times$ year.
    Available at \url{https://atlas.cid.harvard.edu/}.
  \item[\textbf{Simulated data (testing)}]
    {\sloppy Use \fn{ec.make\_sample\_data()} to generate a synthetic
    DataFrame; \fn{n\_locs}, \fn{n\_acts} and \fn{seed} set its size and
    the random seed.\par}
\end{description}

\subsection{Minimal Execution Script}

Ready-to-use template for any CSV-format dataset:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Minimal template --- copy and adapt}]
"""
Usage template for the econcomplex library.
Adapt the column names and the CSV file path.
"""
import pandas as pd
import econcomplex as ec

# 1. Load data
df = pd.read_csv("data.csv")
print("Columns:", df.columns.tolist())
print("Shape:", df.shape)

# 2. Compute all indicators
result = ec.compute_complexity(
    df,
    cols={
        "loc":  "region_column_name",    # e.g.: "municipality", "state"
        "act":  "activity_column_name",  # e.g.: "cnae", "product"
        "val":  "value_column_name",     # e.g.: "jobs", "exports"
        "time": "year_column_name",      # optional
    },
    method="eigenvector",   # 'eigenvector' | 'reflections' | 'fitness'
    threshold=1.0,
    compute_coi_cog=True,
)

# 3. Export results
result.to_csv("complexity_results.csv", index=False)
print("Result saved to complexity_results.csv")
print("Generated columns:", sorted(result.columns.tolist()))

# 4. Individual indicators (optional)
mat = ec.pivot_to_matrix(df,
                         "region_column_name",
                         "activity_column_name",
                         "value_column_name")

eci, pci  = ec.eci_pci(mat)
hachman   = ec.hachman_index(mat)
krugman   = ec.krugman_index(mat)
entropy   = ec.shannon_entropy(mat)

output = pd.DataFrame({
    "eci": eci, "hachman": hachman,
    "krugman": krugman, "entropy": entropy,
})
print(output.round(3))
\end{lstlisting}

% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- --
\subsection{Running with RAIS / CNAE Data (real use case)}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Usage with RAIS data --- municipalities x CNAE}]
import pandas as pd
import econcomplex as ec

# Load aggregated RAIS
# Expected columns: municipality, cnae2d, year, jobs
rais = pd.read_parquet("rais_municipality_cnae.parquet")

# Filter a single year for analysis
rais_2022 = rais[rais["year"] == 2022].copy()

# Pipeline
result = ec.compute_complexity(
    rais_2022,
    cols={"loc": "municipality", "act": "cnae2d", "val": "jobs"},
    method="eigenvector",
    compute_coi_cog=False,   # disable COG for large datasets
)

# Top 20 most complex municipalities
top20 = (result[["municipality", "eci"]]
         .drop_duplicates()
         .sort_values("eci", ascending=False)
         .head(20))
print(top20.to_string(index=False))

result.to_csv("complexity_municipalities_2022.csv", index=False)
\end{lstlisting}

# rais with "year" column containing 2010, 2015, 2020
panel_result = ec.compute_complexity(
    rais,
    cols={

\section{Economic Complexity (\texttt{complexity})}

\subsection{Eigenvector Method (Hidalgo \& Hausmann, 2009)}

\[
  \text{ECI} = \frac{\vec{v}_2 - \mu(\vec{v}_2)}{\sigma(\vec{v}_2)}, \quad
  \text{PCI} = \frac{\vec{u}_2 - \mu(\vec{u}_2)}{\sigma(\vec{u}_2)}
\]
where $\vec{v}_2$ is the second left eigenvector of matrix $\tilde{M}$:
\[
  \tilde{M}_{ij} = \frac{M_{ij}}{k_{c,0} \cdot k_{p,0}}
\]

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={ECI and PCI using the eigenvector method}]

print(pci.head())   # Series indexed by product/sector

\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{GENEPY (Sciarra et al., 2020)}

\begin{sloppypar}
GENEPY reconciles the two views of complexity. The binary matrix is
rescaled into $W_{cp} = M_{cp} / (k_c\,k'_p)$, with $k_c$ the diversity
and $k'_p = \sum_c M_{cp}/k_c$; the proximity between locations is
$N = WW^{\top}$ with a zero diagonal. With $X_1, X_2$ its two leading
eigenvectors and $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ their eigenvalues,
\end{sloppypar}

\[
  \text{GENEPY}_c = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{2} \lambda_i X_{c,i}^2\Big)^{2}
                    + 2 \sum_{i=1}^{2} \lambda_i^{2} X_{c,i}^{2}
\]

The first eigenvector is a linearized Fitness and the second is related
to the ECI. The implementation follows the code released by the authors
(the two eigenvalues largest in absolute value) and is tested against a
transcription of it.

\subsection{ECI Diagnostics}

\begin{sloppypar}
The eigenvector ECI splits the locations along the main axis of
difference in what they do (Mealy, Farmer \& Teytelboym, 2019). In small
or sparse subnational matrices that split may be neither clear nor
stable. \fn{eci\_diagnostics} reports the spectral gap between the
second and third eigenvalues, the correlation of the ECI with diversity,
and the stability of the ranking when the ECI is recomputed on random
subsets of the activities.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{warnbox}
  An eigenvector has no sign of its own: the library orients the ECI so
  that it correlates positively with diversity. Mealy et al.\ find that
  correlation to be positive for countries, absent for US states and
  negative for UK local authorities. When \texttt{corr\_diversity} is
  weak, check the orientation against an outside variable (income,
  wages) before reading ``high ECI'' as ``complex''.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={GENEPY and ECI diagnostics}]
g_regions, g_activities = ec.eci_pci(mat, method="genepy")

diag = ec.eci_diagnostics(mat, n_boot=100, seed=1)
diag[["gap", "corr_diversity", "rank_stability_mean", "sign_flips"]]
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Explaining and Decomposing Density}

\begin{sloppypar}
Density is a sum over the activities the location already has, so it can
be opened up. \fn{density\_contributions} lists how much each present
activity $k$ adds to the density of one pair,
$M_{ik}\,\phi_{kj} / \sum_k \phi_{kj}$; the contributions add up to
$\omega_{ij}$ and give the plain-language reason for an opportunity
(``$j$ is close because the region already does A, B and C'').
\end{sloppypar}

Density is also mechanically higher in diversified locations and varies
with the position of the activity in the network.
\fn{decomposed\_density} removes both level effects and keeps what is
specific to the pair --- the residual of the two-way fixed-effects
regression $\omega_{ij} = a_i + b_j + e_{ij}$, which on a complete matrix
is $e_{ij} = \omega_{ij} - \bar\omega_{i\cdot} - \bar\omega_{\cdot j} +
\bar\omega$. \texttt{NaN} cells are left out of the estimation. Nomaler
\& Verspagen (2024) regress density on the diversity of the location and
the ubiquity of the activity and call the residual ``related variety'';
the fixed effects used here absorb both, together with any other
location- or activity-level factor.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Contributions to density and density net of fixed effects}]
contrib = ec.density_contributions(mat, "my_region", "target",
                                   phi=phi_d)
contrib.head(5)   # activities that pull the target closer
contrib.sum()     # equals the density of the pair

specific = ec.decomposed_density(ec.density(mat, phi=phi_d))
# fixed effects estimated only on the activities each region lacks
external = ec.relatedness_density_external(mat, phi=phi_d)
specific_ext = ec.decomposed_density(external)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Skill Relatedness (flows between activities)}

Two activities are related when workers move between them more often
than expected (Neffke \& Henning, 2013; Neffke, Otto \& Weyh, 2017).
With $F_{ij}$ the flow from activity $i$ to activity $j$:

\[
  SR_{ij} = \frac{F_{ij}}{F_{i\cdot}\,F_{\cdot j} / F_{\cdot\cdot}},
  \qquad
  \widetilde{SR}_{ij} = \frac{SR_{ij} - 1}{SR_{ij} + 1} \in [-1, 1)
\]

Zero means ``as many moves as expected''. The measure is directed;
\texttt{symmetric=True} averages the two directions after the
transformation, as in Neffke, Otto \& Weyh (2017). The same function works for any origin $\times$
destination count (occupations, flows between regions).

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Skill relatedness from job switches}]
# long table of job switches: origin, destination, workers
flows  = ec.pivot_to_matrix(switches, "origin", "destination",
                            "workers")
sr     = ec.skill_relatedness(flows)                  # directed
sr_sym = ec.skill_relatedness(flows, symmetric=True)  # undirected

# as a proximity matrix: pairs with more flows than expected
phi_skill = sr_sym.clip(lower=0).fillna(0)
density_skill = ec.density(mat, phi=phi_skill)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Spatial Density}

The spatial counterpart of density: the share of a location's neighbours
that are already specialized in the activity (Bahar, Hausmann \& Hidalgo,
2014; Boschma, Mart\'{i}n \& Minondo, 2017),

\[
  SD_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{i'} w_{ii'}\,M_{i'j}}{\sum_{i'} w_{ii'}}
\]

The weights $w$ are supplied by the user (contiguity, inverse distance,
commuting flows), so no geographic library is required. Locations
without neighbours get \texttt{NaN}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Spatial density}]
# W: regions x regions (contiguity, inverse distance, flows...)
sd = ec.spatial_density(mat, W)   # share of neighbours, 0 to 1
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Implied Comparative Advantage and Input-Output Relatedness}

\begin{sloppypar}
\fn{implied\_rca} (Hausmann, Stock \& Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m, 2022) predicts the
RCA of a location in an activity from what similar units do, using the
intensities themselves instead of a binary presence. Similarity is
$(1 + \text{corr})/2$ of the RCA vectors (capped at 5 for this step
only); the prediction is the similarity-weighted mean over the $k$
nearest neighbours ($k = \sqrt{N}$ by default), the unit itself
excluded. \texttt{space="activity"} averages over similar activities
within the location, \texttt{space="region"} over similar locations
within the activity. A cell far below its implied value tends to grow.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{sloppypar}
\fn{io\_relatedness} builds a proximity matrix from an input-output
table: the share of industry $i$'s intermediate sales going to $j$,
averaged with the reverse direction,
$IOR_{ij} = \tfrac12\,(Z_{ij}/Z_{i\cdot} + Z_{ji}/Z_{j\cdot})$
(Essletzbichler, 2015, after Fan \& Lang, 2000). Purchase shares and the
maximum of the two directions are available as options.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Implied RCA and input-output relatedness}]
implied_act = ec.implied_rca(mat)                  # similar activities
implied_reg = ec.implied_rca(mat, space="region")  # similar regions
gap = implied_act - ec.rca(mat)       # > 0: below what peers suggest

# Z: sellers x buyers block of an input-output table
phi_io = ec.io_relatedness(Z)                       # (sales shares, mean)
density_io = ec.density(mat, phi=phi_io)
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Complexity Outlook (\texttt{outlook})}

The \textbf{COI} measures the latent diversification potential;
the \textbf{COG} measures the expected complexity gain:

\[
  \text{COI}_c = \sum_p (1 - M_{cp})\,\omega_{cp}\,Q_p
\]

\[
  \text{COG}_{cp} = (1 - M_{cp}) \sum_{p'} (1 - M_{cp'})\,
     \frac{\phi_{pp'}}{\sum_{p''} \phi_{p''p'}}\,Q_{p'}
\]

Entering $p$ raises the density of every other missing activity $p'$ by
$\phi_{pp'}/\sum_{p''}\phi_{p''p'}$, and each gain is valued at $Q_{p'}$:
COG is the resulting increase in COI (gross of the term of $p$ itself,
which leaves the sum).

\begin{notebox}
  \textbf{Changed in 1.2.0.} Up to version 1.2.0 the COG was normalized
  by the total proximity of the target activity $p$ instead of that of
  each $p'$. The two only coincide when all activities have the same
  total proximity; values change slightly (correlation around 0.97 with
  the previous ones on the sample data). The current form matches the
  Growth Lab's \texttt{py-ecomplexity} and is tested against the actual
  change in COI.
\end{notebox}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={COI and COG}]
coi = ec.coi(mat, pci, phi=phi_d)
cog = ec.cog(mat, pci, phi=phi_d)
\end{lstlisting}

\section{ECI Optimization (\texttt{optimization})}

A target-oriented optimization layer for strategic diversification
(Stojkoski \& Hidalgo, 2026, \emph{Research Policy} 55, 105454).
A stepping-stone forecast model is calibrated on a historical panel,
the effort $W_{cp}$ (the added RCA an economy needs to enter an
activity) is obtained in closed form, and a 0--1 integer program
selects the minimal-effort portfolio of new activities that reaches a
target ECI.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={ECI Optimization workflow}]
# 1. Calibrate the stepping-stone model on a panel (t, t+5, t+10)
model = ec.calibrate_steppingstone(df, "region", "sector", "jobs",
                                   "year", horizon=10, steppingstone=5)

# 2. Effort and no-policy forecast for the latest year
W  = ec.effort_matrix(mat, model)            # added RCA per candidate
fc = ec.forecast_specialization(mat, model)  # rca, mcp, pci, eci

# 3. Minimal-effort portfolio raising each location's ECI by 0.1.
#    time_limit caps the exact solver per location (default 5 s);
#    the `optimal` column says whether optimality was proven.
portfolio = ec.eci_optimization(mat, model, delta_eci=0.1)

# 4. Optional: growth targeting - convert a growth target (3.5%/yr)
#    into an ECI target and optimize towards it
gm = ec.calibrate_growth_model(panel, "region", "year", "gdppc", "eci")
eci_star  = ec.eci_target_for_growth(gm, 0.035, gdppc_now)
portfolio = ec.eci_optimization(mat, model, target_eci=eci_star)
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Strategic Diffusion (\texttt{optimization.diffusion})}

Complex-contagion model of diversification on the network of related
activities (Alshamsi, Pinheiro \& Hidalgo, 2018, \emph{Nature
Communications} 9, 1328): the probability of entering an activity is
$p_i = B\,(\text{fraction of related activities present})^{\alpha}$.
The greedy strategy of always entering the most related activity is
suboptimal; minimal diversification time requires targeting hubs in a
narrow early window.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Strategic diffusion workflow}]
adj = ec.proximity_network(mat, phi_threshold=0.55)  # product space
fit = ec.calibrate_contagion(df, "region", "sector", "jobs", "year",
                             adjacency=adj)          # B and alpha

active0 = ec.mcp(mat).loc["my_region"]               # current portfolio
seq = ec.diversification_strategy(adj, active0, B=fit["B"],
                                  alpha=fit["alpha"], strategy="greedy")
table = ec.compare_strategies(adj, active0, B=fit["B"], alpha=fit["alpha"])
best  = ec.optimize_sequence(adj, active0, B=fit["B"], alpha=fit["alpha"])
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Strategy: Desirability and Quadrants (\texttt{strategy})}

Choosing diversification targets takes two questions: what is within
reach (\emph{feasibility} --- density, relative relatedness, a predicted
probability) and what is worth having (\emph{desirability}). This module
makes the second explicit and combines the two.

\fn{desirability\_index} puts each indicator on a common scale (z-score
by default) and takes the weighted mean,
$D_j = \sum_k w_k z_{jk} / \sum_k |w_k|$. The weights are a normative
choice and should be reported; \fn{weight\_sensitivity} redraws them from
a Dirichlet distribution centred on the chosen weights and shows how far
each activity's rank moves. \fn{strategy\_quadrants} then classifies the
options of each location, with cuts computed inside the location
(Balland et al., 2019; Pinheiro et al., 2022):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Quadrant} & \textbf{Feasibility} & \textbf{Desirability} \\
\midrule
\texttt{short jump} & above the cut & above the cut \\
\texttt{long bet}   & below         & above \\
\texttt{low value}  & above         & below \\
\texttt{avoid}      & below         & below \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Desirability, sensitivity to the weights and quadrants}]
# one column per indicator, one row per activity
indicators = pd.DataFrame({"pci": pci, "wage": wage, "pgi": pgi})
weights    = {"pci": 0.5, "wage": 0.3, "pgi": -0.2}  # negative: against

desirability = ec.desirability_index(indicators, weights)
robust = ec.weight_sensitivity(indicators, weights, n_draws=1000,
                               top=20, seed=1)
# robust["top_share"]: share of the weightings with the activity
# among the 20 best

# NaN where the activity is already present; cuts by region
options   = ec.relatedness_density_external(mat, phi=phi_d)
quadrants = ec.strategy_quadrants(options, desirability)
quadrants.loc["my_region"].value_counts()
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{sloppypar}
\fn{opportunity\_alignment} summarizes the picture per location: the
correlation, across its options, between feasibility and desirability.
Negative values flag locations whose nearby opportunities are of low
value. It formalizes a comparison that Mealy \& Coyle (2022) make
visually; part of the pattern is mechanical (high-complexity places have
complex activities nearby), so compare places of similar complexity.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Alignment between feasibility and desirability}]
# > 0: nearby options are also the valuable ones; < 0: long bets needed
alignment = ec.opportunity_alignment(options, desirability)
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Policy Lists (\texttt{policy})}

A \emph{list} is any set of activities a policy cares about: green
products, the industries of a mission or of a value chain, activities
exposed to a transition. Lists are passed as
\texttt{\{name: [activities]\}} or as a boolean table (activities
$\times$ lists).

\fn{list\_complexity\_potential} computes, for each location $c$ and
list $L$,

\[
  \text{presence}_{cL} = \sum_{p \in L} M_{cp}\,v_p, \qquad
  \text{potential}_{cL} = \frac{1}{|L|} \sum_{p \in L} (1 - M_{cp})\,f_{cp}\,v_p
\]

\begin{sloppypar}
with $v$ a value per activity (PCI, wage) and $f$ a feasibility measure.
It generalizes the Green Complexity Index and Potential of Mealy \&
Teytelboym (2022) to any list, value and feasibility. Their potential is
the mean over the green products the country lacks, with PCI rescaled to
$[0,1]$: use \texttt{average="missing"} and
\texttt{value=ec.normalize\_01(pci)}. With the list of all activities
and $f$ = density, $\text{potential} \times |L|$ is the COI. \fn{list\_density} returns the part of the density that rests on the
activities of each list (same denominator as the density, so the parts
are additive). \fn{missing\_links} groups the activities of a chain into
links and reports, per location, which links are present, feasible or
missing.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Indicators for lists of activities}]
lists = {"green": green_codes, "mission_health": health_codes}

# feasibility: density as a share, or a predicted probability
feas = ec.density(mat, phi=phi_d, percent=False)
tab  = ec.list_complexity_potential(mat, pci, feas, lists)
tab["potential"].unstack()                  # regions x lists

# density that rests on brown activities (R x C)
parts = ec.list_density(mat, {"brown": brown_codes}, phi=phi_d)
parts["brown"]

chain = {"inputs": [...], "components": [...], "assembly": [...]}
ec.missing_links(mat, chain, feas, cut=0.25)
# status of each link: present / feasible / missing
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{sloppypar}
\fn{transition\_risk} measures the exposure of each location to a list
of activities expected to shrink --- the ``brown'' list of Andres et
al.\ (2023) --- and how easily it can move out. With $s_{cb}$ the share
of activity $b$ in the location's total value and $\widetilde{Q}_b$ the
PCI rescaled to $[0,1]$:
\end{sloppypar}

\[
  \text{exposure}_c = \sum_{b} s_{cb}, \qquad
  \text{lock-in}_c = \sum_{b} s_{cb}\,(1 - \widetilde{Q}_b), \qquad
  TO_b = \frac{\operatorname{mean}_{q \in T}\,\phi_{bq}}
              {\operatorname{mean}_{p}\,\phi_{bp}}
\]

The \emph{outlook} of a location is the mean of $TO_b$ over the exposed
activities it is specialized in; above 1, they are closer to the target
list $T$ (green activities, or everything not exposed) than to the
average activity.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Exposure, lock-in and transition outlook}]
risk = ec.transition_risk(mat, brown_codes, pci=pci,
                          targets=green_codes, phi=phi_d)
risk.sort_values("lock_in", ascending=False).head()
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Entry Prediction and Validation (\texttt{prediction})}

Relatedness is useful for policy to the extent that it predicts where
locations actually diversify. This module tests that out of sample, for
any predictor.

\fn{entry\_targets} defines the event: a cell is \emph{at risk} when
$\text{RCA}_{t_0} < 0.5$ and \emph{enters} when $\text{RCA}_{t_1} \geq 1$
(both thresholds are parameters; a minimum size can be required). The gap
between the two thresholds avoids counting cells that hover around
RCA $= 1$.

\fn{validate\_entry} compares one or more score matrices with the
target. A score is anything in which higher means ``more likely to
enter'': density, relative relatedness, a model's predicted probability,
the initial RCA, a rule of thumb.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\small
\begin{tabular}{l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{10.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Reading} \\
\midrule
\texttt{auc} & Pooled ROC AUC over all cells at risk; 0.5 = no information. \\
\texttt{auc\_within} & Mean of the AUC computed inside each location: the
  ability to rank the options \emph{of a given location}, free of
  differences in size and diversification between locations. \\
\texttt{average\_precision} & Area under the precision--recall curve;
  informative when entries are rare. \\
\texttt{precision@k} & Share of a location's $k$ best-ranked options that
  entered. \\
\texttt{recall@k} & Share of a location's entries found among its $k$
  best-ranked options. \\
\texttt{brier} & Mean squared error of the score as a probability
  (only for scores in $[0,1]$). \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

With \texttt{n\_boot} $> 0$ the function adds 95\,\% intervals from a
bootstrap of locations, and with \texttt{baseline} the difference of
every metric to a reference predictor.

\begin{warnbox}
  For an honest test, every score --- including the proximity matrix
  behind a density --- must be computed with data up to $t_0$ only, and
  model predictions must be out of sample.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Do the scores predict entry?}]
# everything on the right-hand side uses data up to t0 only
rca0, rca1 = ec.rca(mat_t0), ec.rca(mat_t1)
target, at_risk = ec.entry_targets(rca0, rca1, values_t1=mat_t1,
                                   min_value=10)

phi0   = ec.product_proximity(mat_t0)
scores = {"density":     ec.density(mat_t0, phi=phi0),
          "relative":    ec.relative_relatedness(mat_t0, phi=phi0),
          "persistence": rca0}
val = ec.validate_entry(scores, target, k=(10, 20),
                        baseline="persistence", n_boot=200, seed=1)
val["value"].unstack()   # scores x metrics
val.loc["density"]       # value, 95% interval, diff. to baseline
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Machine-Learning Prediction (optional)}

\begin{sloppypar}
With scikit-learn installed (\texttt{pip install econcomplex[ml]}) the
library also fits prediction models. \fn{build\_features} stacks any
number of location $\times$ activity matrices --- densities computed
with different proximity matrices, spatial density, implied RCA --- into
a table of cells and adds controls (initial RCA, size and diversity of
the location, size and ubiquity of the activity). \fn{predict\_entry}
returns the probability of entry \emph{out of sample}: locations are
split into folds and each cell is predicted by a model that never saw
its location, so the result goes straight into \fn{validate\_entry}
next to the plain densities. Models: gradient boosting (default), random
forest, logistic regression (the transparent benchmark) or any
scikit-learn classifier. \texttt{mode="vector"} fits one model per
activity on the location's whole RCA vector, with no proximity matrix
(Tacchella et al., 2023; Albora et al., 2023).
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{sloppypar}
\fn{explain\_entry} reports which features carry the prediction:
permutation importance (fall in AUC) on held-out locations, with a
p-value. \fn{potential\_employment} turns calibrated probabilities into
quantities, $\text{prob}_{cp} \times \text{arrival}_{cp}$, where the
arrival size is the one that gives a location quotient of 1
($E_{c\cdot}E_{\cdot p}/E_{\cdot\cdot}$) or a quantile of what past
entrants reached.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={From densities to a prediction model and expected jobs}]
# pip install econcomplex[ml]
layers = {"density": ec.density(mat_t0, phi=phi0),
          "implied": ec.implied_rca(mat_t0),
          "spatial": ec.spatial_density(mat_t0, W)}
X = ec.build_features(mat_t0, layers, at_risk=at_risk)

# out of sample: each region is predicted by a model that never saw it
scores["gbm"]   = ec.predict_entry(X, target, model="gbm", seed=1)
scores["logit"] = ec.predict_entry(X, target, model="logit", seed=1)
ec.validate_entry(scores, target, baseline="density")["value"].unstack()

ec.explain_entry(X, target, seed=1)   # which layers carry the prediction

# next window: fit on t0 -> t1, predict from the features observed at t1
X_t1 = ec.build_features(mat_t1, layers_t1, at_risk=ec.rca(mat_t1) < 0.5)
prob = ec.predict_entry(X, target, new_features=X_t1, calibrate=True)
jobs = ec.potential_employment(prob, mat_t1)        # arrival at LQ = 1
jobs.groupby("region")["expected"].sum()
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{notebox}
  \texttt{compute\_complexity} is a thin \textbf{orchestration layer}: it
  calls the same public functions you would call by hand (\fn{rca},
  \fn{mcp}, \fn{diversity}, \fn{ubiquity}, \fn{eci\_pci}, \fn{proximity},
  \fn{relatedness\_density}, \fn{distance}, \fn{coi}/\fn{cog}) and merges
  the results back into long format, looping over periods for panels. It
  is the convenience entry point for the common case; for fine-grained
  control, call the matrix-level functions directly.
\end{notebox}

\section{Interpreting the Indicators}

Quick-reference guide to reading the main outputs. All complexity
indices are \textbf{relative measures}: compare them only within the
same matrix (same period, same activity classification).

{\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\small
\begin{longtable}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.4cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.0cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{8.6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Indicator} & \textbf{Typical range} & \textbf{Reading} \\
\midrule\endhead
ECI / PCI & z-score (mean 0, sd 1) & Higher = more complex location /
  activity. \texttt{NaN} = unit trimmed for zero diversity/ubiquity. \\
Fitness / Complexity & $> 0$ (or log scale) & Non-linear analogue of
  ECI/PCI; ranking is the meaningful output. \\
RCA / LQ & $\geq 0$; 1 = neutral & $>1$: location specialized in the
  activity; basis of the binary matrix $M_{cp}$. \\
Proximity $\phi$ & $[0, 1]$ & Probability-like similarity between
  activities; higher = easier joint specialization. \\
Density $\omega$ & 0--100\,\% & Share of the activities related to $c$
  that the location already does; higher = easier entry. \\
Relative relatedness $\tilde\omega$ & z-score & $>0$: activity more
  related than the average of the location's option set. \\
Distance & $[0, 1]$ & $1-\omega/100$; higher = harder entry. \\
COI & unbounded & Higher = more complex activities within reach
  (diversification potential). \\
COG & $\geq 0$ per activity & Gain in COI if the location develops the
  activity; identifies strategic bets. \\
Skill relatedness & $[-1, 1)$ & $>0$: more flows between the two
  activities than expected; $-1$: no flow. \\
Spatial density & $[0, 1]$ & Share of the neighbours already specialized
  in the activity. \\
Decomposed density & residual (mean 0) & $>0$: the pair is closer than
  the location's and the activity's averages explain. \\
Desirability index & z-score units & Higher = more desirable under the
  stated weights; read it with \texttt{weight\_sensitivity}. \\
List potential & units of $v$ & Average value of the list's missing
  activities, discounted by feasibility. \\
AUC (entry) & $[0, 1]$; 0.5 = chance & Probability that an entry is
  ranked above a non-entry.\\
GENEPY & $\geq 0$ & Higher = more complex; combines the Fitness and ECI
  views. Not standardized. \\
Implied RCA & units of RCA & RCA expected from similar activities or
  locations; observed below implied suggests room to grow. \\
Opportunity alignment & $[-1, 1]$ & $<0$: the location's nearby options
  are its least valuable ones. \\
Lock-in / outlook & $[0,1]$ / around 1 & Exposure weighted by low
  complexity; outlook $>1$ = exposed activities close to the targets. \\
Locational Gini & $[0, 0.5]$ & Higher = activity geographically more
  concentrated. \\
HHI (normalized) & $[0, 1]$ & Higher = location's portfolio more
  concentrated. Entropy reads the opposite way. \\
Hachman & $[0, 1]$ & 1 = location mirrors the aggregate structure. \\
Krugman & $[0, 2]$ & Higher = structure more different from the
  aggregate. \\
PRODY / EXPY & units of the income vector & Income level associated
  with an activity / a location's basket. \\
Effort $W_{cp}$ & added RCA & Lower = cheaper entry; $\leq 0$ means the
  forecast model already predicts entry. \\
Contagion $B$, $\alpha$ & $B \in (0,1]$, $\alpha \approx 1$ &
  $\alpha > 1$: related activities reinforce each other (complex
  contagion). \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}}

\begin{warnbox}
  Inputs such as GDP per capita (PRODY/EXPY), population (RPOP), the
  regional Gini vector (PGI), and emissions (PEII) are \textbf{external
  data}: the library does not derive them from the matrix, and proxy
  variables produce indicator values without economic meaning.
\end{warnbox}

import econcomplex as ec

# 1. Panel with at least t, t+5, t+10 (e.g. 2005, 2010, 2015)
rais = pd.read_parquet("rais_municipality_cnae_2005_2015.parquet")

model = ec.calibrate_steppingstone(

    horizon=10, steppingstone=5,

mat = ec.pivot_to_matrix(rais[rais["year"] == 2015],
                         "municipality", "cnae2d", "jobs")

print(portfolio.head())   # location, activity, effort, pci, targets

# 4. Optional: growth targeting (needs GDP per capita and ECI panel)
